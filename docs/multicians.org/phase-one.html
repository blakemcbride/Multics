Loading

Tom Van Vleck

Karolyn Martin and Don Widrig at the GE 645 control panel. MIT Project MAC machine room, 9th floor, Tech Square, April 1968. Photo by THVV. Click for a larger view.

We sort of made up project management as we went along at Project MAC. (Corby and Charlie Clingen wrote a great paper on the management challenges of the project.) One technique we used was to define "phases," each of which had quality targets and a functional benchmark for the integrated system. (In retrospect, we should have set tight performance goals too.) Phase One required Multics to bootload on the GE 645, create a full-fledged process, and do terminal I/O. It was preceded by Phase 0.5, which demonstrated a working Multics file system on the 645 simulator: that happened in March 1967.

Phase One was planned to happen a few months later, but it took until December 1967 to reach the milestone. Noel Morris and I were asked to take over development and debugging of the Multics bootstrap program designed by Andre Bensoussan and Nguyen Van Binh. This project evolved into our implementing the complete boot sequence for Multics. A lot had to be integrated to make Phase One run, and we fell into a mode of serial debugging of each component. Each development run required editing on a heavily loaded CTSS, painfully slow EPL compilation, complete regeneration of the system boot tape, and an attempt to boot the system. (The system tape didn't contain a linked image of the OS, the way other systems did: our design was that every site's system tape would be identical, and that the system would configure and link itself during the boot process. This elegant goal wasn't easy to design.) Our boot attempts would eventually crash, and then a programmer would take a core dump on the printer, take it to his or her desk, and debug so that the whole cycle could start again. (John Gintell wrote a slick dump program that booted from the card reader. A full dump in those days was only a side inch of paper or so; as the system grew, dumps did too, till they were over a foot high.)

The Phase One team could only get one run a day on first shift, so Noel and I worked second and sometimes even third shifts. There was pressure and anxiety, personality conflicts, and stupidity; but we also had brilliant programmers, teamwork, good times, camaraderie, and pride in working on the biggest challenge in the computer field.

For each bootload run, we would generate a new Multics System Tape, take it to the GE-645 in the machine room on the 9th floor of Tech Square, configure the GE-645, and try to boot from the tape. At first, Multics would crash right away, in the program bootstrap1. We'd take a comre dump and analyze where it crashed and why. Sometimes we could figure out what to fix, recompile a program or two, generate another MST, and try again. Other times, Multics would crash in a program created by some other team member, and we would have to leave notes and a core dump for the day-shift team, and hope they could find and fix the failure so that we could make a new system tape for the next run.

The first time bootstrap1 succeded and called initialzer, the program that sequenced the first phase of initialization, we had an awful realization: the "Multics way" to make this transfer would be to take a linkage fault that would be caught by the Fault Interceptor Module which would invoke the program link_fault to find initialzer and "snap" the link. But initialzer used other supervisor routines to do this -- and they were not linked. The system would take an endless chain of linkage faults while trying to service a linkage fault. As described in Bootstrap Loading, we had to hurriedly invent a facility called the prelinker that would do more or less what a traditional batch system loader did: that is, find every unsnapped link in "collection 1" of the just-loaded supervisor and snap the link if its target had also been loaded by bootstrap1. To make this work, we had to build machinery used only until the dynamic linkage mechanism was set up.

(Fred Brooks wrote about the "second system effect" in The Mythical Man-Month but we didn't know then that we should avoid it; Multics was our first second system. Contrary to some writers' views, the effect isn't one of going crazy adding features to the design of the second system; instead the designers of a second system want to do it really right this time; they want to meet more or less the same requirements, but this time with elegance and completeness. What happens is that features that were added on in a crockish way on the first system get provided for in the basic design of the second one. But inserting all this elegance and flexibility makes it harder to make design decisions, and harder to end up with a simple design that has a chance of performing well and integrating quickly. In other words, the complexity gets moved back from implementation and maintenance, as it was in the first system, into design complexity in the second system.)

The goal of Phase One was to boot the system from tape on a cold GE-645, with no help from GECOS; load and initialize the whole Multics supervisor and file system; create a full Multics process providing paged, segmented virtual memory and dynamic linking; and execute commands from the operator's console, a Model 37 Teletype in the machine room.

Noel and I made the bootload run that finally achieved Phase One in December 1967. After booting, the Model 37 TTY typed out 

  Multics is the one system to have when you're having more than one.

(This was a parody of a beer commercial of the time; the point was (a) this was our second system -- CTSS already worked and we were using it to build Multics; (b) Multics was designed to run more than one CPU. Well, we thought it was funny.)

It was late at night, maybe ten or eleven, and we debated whether to call Corby at home or to let it wait till morning. We decided to call him & he was very polite, considering the hour.

(The picture above, of Don and Karolyn debugging, doesn't show the headphones. The 645 was so big, occupied so much floor space, that it was hard to debug. I am pretty sure that Don is looking pensively at one of the CPUs, and the other one is in the background. The cabinets were 7 feet high, 3 feet wide, and about 10 feet deep. But for system bringup debugging, we also had to look at the control panel for the GIOC, a truly huge monster, X-shaped like four CPUs back to back to back to back. And sometimes we needed to station somebody by the controller for the firehose drum as well. Somebody got the bright idea that we should have an intercom system, and installed one inside the various cabinets. There were several sets of earphones with microphone attached, hooked to coil cables with phone plugs at the end. You could put on a headset and plug in at whatever cabinet you were looking at, and talk to a colleague three or four boxes away. There were even two intercom circuits, A and B, selected by a switch on each box, so that two teams could be debugging different hardware groupings at the same time. I think we used the stuff about three times. Being tethered to the machine was a pain; it was easier to walk around a few boxes than to mess with the intercom. The wiring for the intercom was removed a few years later, about 1969, when "bad grounding" was blamed for repeated hardware crashes, and Field Engineering decided to remove all the wires that weren't in the prints.)

(Speaking of beer commercials. Sometime in the 70s, there was a big billboard in Kendall square that proclaimed, "Fresh Beer Is Like Fresh Anything." I was never sure how to take this. Was this an obvious remark, or a subtle explanation of how we understand words, or just marketing? Anyway it made me happy.)
